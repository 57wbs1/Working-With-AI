Lesson 03

From answering to doing

How we got here, and what “agentic” actually means.

CLIP 01Also generated. Six seconds, no audio, about thirty cents.

Bottom line The jump that matters was not a cleverer chatbot. It was the day the model stopped handing you an answer and started taking actions in a loop until the job was done. You went from writing tasks to giving intent.
How it got here
BEFORE 2017
Narrow models, hand-built

One model, one job, trained by specialists on a labelled dataset. Spam filters, image classifiers. Useless outside the task it was cut for.

2017
The transformer

A Google paper, Attention Is All You Need, proposes an architecture that scales with data and compute instead of plateauing. Everything since is downstream of it.

2018–2021
Scale as a strategy

Train one large model on most of the internet, then adapt it. GPT-2, GPT-3. Impressive in demos, awkward to use, no interface the public cared about.

NOV 2022
ChatGPT, the interface moment

The capability was mostly already there. Putting a chat box on it is what made it a household object. Remember that: interface changes adoption more than capability does.

2023–2024
Tools, context, modality

Models learn to call functions, read images, and hold a book-length context. Still fundamentally one question in, one answer out.

LATE 2024
MCP, a standard plug

Anthropic publishes the Model Context Protocol and opens it. One way to connect any model to any tool or data source, instead of a bespoke integration per pairing. Section 04.

2025–2026
Agents, and the shift in your job

Reasoning models that think before answering, coding agents that work unattended for hours, skills, agent teams, cloud runners. Your work moves from writing the steps to writing the intent and checking the result.

What “agentic” actually means

The word does a lot of marketing work. Here is the honest ladder. Most people sit on rung one and assume that is the ceiling. Click each rung.

L1 ChatbotYou ask · it answers · you do the work OPEN

It produces text. Nothing happens in the world. You still open the file, check the maths and send the email. A very fast writer with no hands.

Looks like: “Draft me a paragraph on X.” You copy it out.
L2 CopilotIt suggests inside your tool · you still drive OPEN

It sits where you work and completes what you started. Faster, but the loop is unchanged: it proposes, you accept or reject, every time. You are the bottleneck by design.

Looks like: autocomplete in your editor, Smart Compose in mail.
L3 Tool useIt can reach out · one step at a time OPEN

You hand it a set of things it is allowed to call — search the web, read this file, query that sheet. It picks the right one and uses it. Still one hop: you ask, it fetches, it answers.

Looks like: “What is in my calendar on Thursday?” and it genuinely looks.
L4 AgenticYou give intent · it loops until done OPEN

You state an outcome and the constraints. It picks the steps, uses tools, looks at what came back, notices it was wrong, and keeps going until the outcome holds or it gets stuck. You review the result, not the keystrokes.

Four things make it agentic, and all four have to be present: a goal instead of a task, tools it may actually use, a loop where it sees the consequence of its own last move, and memory of what it already tried.

Looks like: “Work out why the sync broke last Tuesday and fix it.” Forty minutes later: a diagnosis, a patch, and a list of what it could not verify.

You appear twice, and only twice. Everything between is the loop.

What you actually are now

The most useful way to think about an agent is as a staff officer: capable, fast, tireless, well-read, and wholly dependent on you for direction and for judgement about what matters.

So your job is the one you already know. You are not operating a machine, you are commanding one: setting intent, bounding it, then reviewing the product before it goes anywhere. That review is now the main thing you are paid for.

A good staff officer and a good agent fail in the same ways
A staff officer who…An agent that…The same fix

The part that should worry you

A staff officer who is wrong usually shows it. They hesitate, they hedge, they come back with questions. An agent that is wrong reads exactly like an agent that is right.

The signals you spent a career learning to read are not available here. Verify anything before it leaves your hands.

The honest part

An agent that loops can be confidently wrong for forty minutes instead of thirty seconds. It will invent a source, delete the wrong file, or declare success on something it never checked.

The discipline is the same as any delegation: state the constraints, make it show its working, and check the output yourself. Never let an agent be the last pair of eyes on anything carrying your name.

Lesson 05

Skills — teaching it your standards

How to stop re-explaining your house style every single time.

CLIP 02Eight things plugging into one core. That is literally section 04.

Bottom line A skill is a folder of written instructions the model loads only when the task matches. Write your standard down once and it applies every time, to everyone you share it with. This is the single highest-leverage thing in the whole brief and almost nobody uses it.
What a skill actually is

A skill is a folder holding written instructions. It sits dormant until a task matches its description, then the model loads it and follows it. Loading happens on demand, so you can keep two hundred skills and only the relevant one ever enters the conversation.

  • Why you care: every standard you repeat, a house format, a checklist, a marking rubric, a brief structure, becomes something you write once and never explain again.
  • Mine: an essay auditor holding the grading criteria and my tutor’s past feedback. I say “audit this” and it runs twenty checks I would otherwise forget.
  • They are plain text. Readable, editable, shareable. Hand your skill to the syndicate and everyone works to the same standard. That is the real unlock for a team.

Two hundred skills cost you nothing until one matches.

Prompting is dead

“Prompt engineering” was a workaround for models that could not follow ordinary instructions. They can now, and that skill has largely expired.

What replaced it is a different job. You stop writing steps and start stating the end state, the constraints, and how you will judge the result. It works out the route.

The old way

INSTRUCTING · YOU OWN EVERY STEP

  • “Act as an expert analyst. First, summarise the attached paper in 200 words. Use formal academic English…”
  • You specify the sequence, so the output can only be as good as your plan
  • Every new task needs the whole instruction written out again
  • It stops the moment it finishes your last step, right or wrong

The agentic way

DELEGATING · YOU OWN THE OUTCOME

  • “I need to know whether this paper undermines my thesis. Tell me what would change my mind.”
  • It picks the steps, and will take ones you would not have thought of
  • Your standards live in a skill, so you never restate them
  • It keeps going until the outcome holds, or tells you it is stuck

Three things to put in every delegation, and they are the same three you would give a capable subordinate:

  1. The end state. What is true when this is done? Not “write a summary” but “I can walk into the tutorial able to defend a position on this.”
  2. The constraints. Word count, sources it may use, house format, what it must not do. These are the bounds, not the route.
  3. The test. How will you both know it worked? A worked example, a check it must pass, a question it must be able to answer.
Why this lands in this room You already know how to do this. It is mission command: intent, constraints, freedom of action, back-brief. The only new part is that the subordinate is a machine, is extremely fast, and will not tell you when it is out of its depth unless you ask.

Before you read on, answer this for yourself: how do you brief a capable junior you cannot supervise directly? Whatever you just thought is the right answer here too.

What one looks like

Not code. A text file in a folder. The top lines say what it is and when to use it, and that is all the model reads until a matching task arrives. A real one, cut down:

--- name: brief-format description: Use when drafting any syndicate brief, deck or slide pack. --- Open every section with a one-line bottom line in bold. Keep slide text telegraphic — drop articles and the verb "is". # "Strike RINGTON", not "The plan is to strike RINGTON" Never use "X, not Y" phrasing. State the claim, then the evidence. Speaking notes in full sentences; slide text in fragments. Mark every deck OFFICIAL (CLOSED) / NON-SENSITIVE.

That is the whole mechanism. The description line is the important part. It is how the model decides whether this skill is relevant to what you just asked. Vague description, skill never fires.

Two worth having on day one

Prosehumanizer

Strips the tells that make writing read as machine-generated: puffed-up significance, triads everywhere, the “not X, but Y” cadence, the em-dash habit, the stock closing paragraph about challenges and future prospects.

It works off a documented catalogue of these patterns rather than vibes, which is why it is worth more than telling the model “write more naturally”, an instruction it cannot actually act on.

Use it onAnything with your name on it that a human will read closely.

Prosestop-slop

The lighter cousin. Removes filler, hedging and over-formality, the “it is worth noting that”, “in today’s rapidly evolving landscape”, “delve into” register that creeps into every draft.

Run it on a draft that is fine but flabby.

Use it onEmails, minutes, anything you want shorter and plainer fast.
A warning about both

Neither makes AI writing undetectable, and that is not what they are for. They make the prose less padded and less formulaic.

If the real question is “can I pass this off as mine”, the answer for assessed work is no. Your institution’s rules apply whatever any tool does.

Skills worth having on a Master’s or graduate diploma

Most published skills are built for software teams and will not help you. The ones that pay off on a taught postgraduate course encode assessment: what a marker rewards, and what loses marks.

Writing your own, the fast route

Do not sit down to write a skill. You will produce something generic. Instead, do the task once properly and let the corrections become the skill.

  1. Do the job once, out loud. Work through a real essay or deck with the model and correct it as you go. Every “no, we always do it this way” is a future rule.
  2. Then say: “turn everything you just learnt into a skill.” It writes the file, including the description line. This is the whole trick, and almost nobody does it.
  3. Cut half of what it wrote. It over-generalises. Keep the rules that are specifically yours; delete the ones that are just good practice, which it already knows.
  4. Sharpen the description line. That line alone decides whether the skill ever fires. Name the trigger: “Use when drafting or reviewing any assessed essay for the X programme.”
  5. Use it and fix what it misses. Mine took four or five rounds before it earned its place.
The payoff, concretely My essay-audit skill holds a marking rubric and every piece of feedback I have been given. I say “audit this” and it runs twenty checks I would otherwise forget at midnight the night before submission. I wrote none of those checks in the moment — they accumulated from corrections I made once.

If a tutor has ever given you written feedback, that feedback is the skill. Most people file it and never open it again. Put it in a skill file and it works on every assignment from now on.

Where to find them
  • Built in. Claude ships with a set — document handling, slide building, spreadsheets. Ask “what skills do you have?” and it will list them.
  • Your organisation’s catalogue. If your workplace publishes skills, they appear in your settings and install in one click.
  • Public repositories. Anthropic publishes a skills repository on GitHub, and there are community collections. Read before you install, a skill is instructions the model will follow, so treat an unknown one the way you would an unknown script.

Lesson 06

MCP, connectors, and where work happens

How the model reaches your tools, and the difference between local and cloud.

Lesson intro · 8 sec

The voice is synthetic too. Worth remembering every time you watch anything online now.

Bottom line MCP is a standard plug that lets any model use any tool. A connector is one of those plugs with the wiring already done. And “local” does not mean private — that distinction is the one that matters most here.
MCP, one plug instead of a hundred adapters

Model Context Protocol. Anthropic published it in late 2024 and gave it away. The problem it solves is unglamorous and enormous.

An MCP server offers three kinds of thing: tools it can do, resources it can read, and prompts it can follow. The model is told what is available and decides when to reach for it. The analogy everyone uses is USB-C, and for once the analogy is fair.

Connectors — MCP with the wiring done
GmailDriveDocsNotionSlackGitHubZotero

A connector is a prepackaged MCP server for a service you already use. You click it, sign in once through the normal login screen, and tick what it is allowed to touch. No files, no terminal.

  • Typical list: Gmail, Google Drive and Calendar, Notion, Slack, GitHub, Figma, Canva.
  • Grant narrowly. Read-only beats read-write unless you need it to write. It can do anything you permit, including the things you did not picture it doing.
  • Revoke when the job is done. A connector you set up for one afternoon should not still be live next term.
Local vs cloud, where the work happens

This trips people up constantly, and the distinction is simpler than it sounds: it is about where the hands are, not where the brain is. The model is a remote service either way. What changes is what it can reach.

Local

ON YOUR MACHINE · e.g. Claude Code, Codex CLI

  • Sees your actual files, folders and version history
  • Runs real commands — installs, tests, builds, scripts
  • Reaches things only your machine can reach
  • Nothing is uploaded except what it chooses to read
  • It can genuinely break things. Keep backups.
  • Needs installing and a little terminal comfort
  • Dies when your laptop sleeps

Cloud

ON THEIR SERVERS · e.g. claude.ai, chatgpt.com

  • Nothing to install, a browser or a phone is enough
  • Sandboxed, so it cannot wreck your machine
  • Keeps running while you are away from the desk
  • Same conversation on every device
  • Sees only what you upload or connect
  • Cannot touch your local files or network
  • Everything you paste leaves your building

Which one for the job in front of you?

Say this part out loud

Local does not mean private. The model still runs on someone else’s servers — local only means the hands are on your machine. Every file it reads locally is sent off to be processed, same as anything you paste into a browser.

So the rule is the boring one and it has not changed: nothing classified, nothing marked, no personal data of other people, into any of these services. Not the free tier, not the paid tier, not the local one. If you would not email it to a vendor, do not prompt it.

Everything in this brief is unclassified, open-source and built on my own time and my own account. Keep it that way and you will not have a bad afternoon.

Lesson 07

Research that stands up

Finding real sources, checking they say what you claim, and digging past the summary.

Lesson intro · 8 sec

Same single photograph as every other clip here. The voice is synthetic as well.

Bottom line A chat model will invent a citation that looks perfect — plausible author, plausible journal, plausible year, and no such paper. The entire discipline here is one rule: never let a citation come from the model. It comes from a search result you can click.
Why invented citations happen

The model generates text likely to follow what came before, and a citation-shaped string is extremely likely to follow a claim. So it produces one: correctly formatted, confidently placed, and attached to nothing.

It happens most when you ask for something that ought to exist. Ask for “a study showing X” and if none is in memory, the likeliest next text is still a citation.

What this costs you

A fabricated reference in assessed work is an academic-integrity problem, and trivially caught: a marker only has to search the title.

The commoner failure is worse. A real paper cited for a claim it does not make survives a quick check and still collapses under a marker who knows the literature.

The workflow that holds up
  1. Map the ground first, with a tool that searches. Perplexity or a Scholar search, something that returns real results with real links. Not a chat window working from memory. You are asking “what exists and who is canonical here?”, not “what do you think?”
  2. Go and find who disagrees. Take your position and search for its strongest published critic by name. A rubric rewards engaging a real opponent and punishes a straw man, and the model will happily build you a straw man if you do not insist otherwise.
  3. Read the actual source for anything load-bearing. Not the summary. If a claim is holding up a section of your argument, you read the page it came from.
  4. Check every citation twice. Once that the paper exists. Again that the specific claim you hung on it is the claim it makes. This second check is where the marks actually are.
  5. Track where each source entered. Note what you took from each and why it is in the essay at all. Any source you cannot justify should be cut, padding the bibliography reads as padding.
Tools that search the real literature

LiteratureGoogle Scholar

Returns actual papers with actual citation counts, and, the useful part, who has cited this since. That forward link is how you find the rebuttal to a paper you were about to lean on.

Connected as a tool, the model searches it and hands back results you can open, rather than recalling titles. The difference between a real and an invented reference is exactly this.

Ask it“Who has cited this and disagreed with it?”, the single most useful research question there is.

LibraryZotero

Holds your sources, PDFs, notes and annotations, and formats the bibliography. Connected as a tool, the model can search your own library, so it answers from papers you have actually collected and read.

That is a meaningful shift: the answer is grounded in your reading list rather than the open web.

Ask it“Which papers in my library contradict each other?”

SourcesGemini Notebook

Upload the readings; it answers only from them, with clickable citations. It cannot wander into half-remembered training data because it has nothing else to draw on.

Ask it“Where do these sources disagree with each other, and on what exactly?”

WebPerplexity

Fast orientation with citations on every claim. Best for the first hour of a topic you know nothing about, who the names are, what the debate is, which terms matter.

Ask it“What is the main line of criticism against this position?”
Digging for the golden nuggets

A summary request gets you a summary: competent, accurate, and worth nothing, because everyone else gets the same one. The value is in questions that force a judgement.

Stop asking the left. Start asking the right.
Instead ofAskWhat it surfaces
“Summarise this paper” “What does this claim that I would have to argue against?” The friction with your own position, the only part you actually need.
“What are the key points?” “What is the weakest link in its argument?” Where to push, and what a marker will already be sceptical about.
“Is my argument good?” “Argue the opposite as strongly as you can.” The counter-case, stated properly instead of set up to lose.
“Find sources on X” “What is the standard objection to X, and who makes it?” A named opponent, which is what engaging the literature means.
“Improve this essay” “Which paragraph is doing the least work? Cut it.” Real edits rather than a light polish of everything.
“Explain this concept” “Where does this concept break down?” The scope conditions, where a distinction class lives.

One more worth memorising: “What would change your answer?” It forces the model to state what its conclusion depends on, which tells you where it is weak, and quite often tells you your own argument rests on something you had not noticed.

Declare it

Every institution now has a position on AI use in assessed work, and they differ sharply — some expect a declaration, some restrict it to specific stages, some prohibit it outright.

Find your programme’s rule and follow it. “I did not know” has never been a defence, and the tools leave more trace than people assume.

Lesson 08

Decks, documents and your own files

Giving it a format to follow, and pointing it at the folders you already have.

Lesson intro · 8 sec

Eight seconds, two minutes of rendering, no studio and no second take.

Bottom line Asking for “a deck on X” gets you generic slides. Giving it your format plus your content gets you something usable. And once it can read your folders, it stops being a chat window and starts being something that knows what you have.
The one move that makes decks work

Do not ask it to design. Ask it to fill a format you have already decided. Design is where it is weakest and where your house style is non-negotiable anyway.

So hand it the rules explicitly. A real set, from the deck standard I work to:

FORMAT 16:9. Minimum 8.5pt type. Never split a point across two slides — trim instead. Yellow bar across the top of each slide = the bottom line, one sentence. SLIDE TEXT Telegraphic. Drop articles and the verb "is". # "Strike RINGTON at first light", not "The plan is to strike RINGTON" No rhetorical antithesis. Never "X, not Y". SPEAKING NOTES Full sentences, written to be read aloud. LENGTH 35–40 visible slides. No repeated content across sections.

That is thirty seconds of typing and it is the difference between a deck you rebuild from scratch and one you tidy. Better still, put it in a skill (Lesson 05) and never type it again.

  • Give it an existing deck as the reference. “Match this one’s structure and tone” works far better than any description you can write.
  • Content first, slides second. Settle the argument in prose, then ask it to lay that out. Decks built before the argument exists are where filler comes from.
  • Make it justify the slide count. “What would you cut to lose five slides?” produces a tighter deck than asking for a shorter one.

Pointing it at your own folders

This is the capability people underestimate most. Running locally (Lesson 06), it can read a whole directory, not one file you uploaded, but everything, with the structure intact.

That changes the kind of question you can ask:

  • “Read every PDF in this folder and build me a thematic index.” Forty readings become a map of which sources speak to which theme.
  • “Which of these documents contradict each other?” Across a folder of orders, notes and drafts, this catches things no one person would spot.
  • “Find every file that mentions this and tell me where it came from.” Search that understands meaning, not just matching strings.
  • “These are my last six essays and their feedback. What do I keep getting wrong?” Genuinely the highest-value question on this page, and it needs the folder to answer.
  • “Reorganise this folder and tell me what you moved.” Useful, and exactly the kind of thing to make it propose before it acts.
Two rules before you point it at a folder

Everything it reads leaves your machine. Local means its hands are local, not that the processing is. A folder of personal data or marked material is not a folder you point anything at.

Make it propose before it moves anything. Add “show me what you would change and wait for me to confirm”. Reading is safe; renaming and deleting are not, and an agent that misunderstands your folder structure is a bad afternoon.

Lesson 10

When it goes wrong

The first output is never right. Knowing why, and when to stop pushing.

Bottom line Nobody gets what they wanted on the first try, and the people who conclude “AI cannot do this” almost always quit at round one. But iteration is not infinite either, and when you start going in circles, the cause is usually the model you are on, not the thing you asked for.
The first output is never right

This is not a defect. It is the same as any brief you have ever given: the first version comes back and you say “not quite, more like this”. The difference is that here the turnaround is ninety seconds instead of three days, so you can afford to do it eight times.

And every round is doing real work. When you say “no, the roster needs to avoid consecutive weekends”, you have not repeated yourself. You have told it something it could not have known. Each correction narrows the target.

This is also why it burns through usage so fast. Tearing the work apart and rebuilding costs roughly what the original build cost. Five rounds is five builds. That is the honest price of the speed, and it is why the usage limits in Lesson 04 bite sooner than people expect.

Worth saying out loud This brief took four substantial rebuilds. A stock presenter became my face. One long page became ten lessons. Four lessons were added after the structure was finished. None of that was failure. It was the work.
Converging, or going in circles?

These look identical for the first few rounds. Learn to tell them apart, because one is worth continuing and the other is worth nothing.

Converging

KEEP GOING

  • Each version is different from the last
  • The thing you named as wrong is now right
  • New problems are smaller than the old ones
  • It asks you a sharp question you had not considered
  • It tells you what it is uncertain about

Circling

STOP — MORE PROMPTING WILL NOT FIX IT

  • A bug you already fixed comes back
  • Same answer, reworded, twice running
  • It agrees with every correction and changes nothing
  • It apologises more than it edits
  • It quietly drops a requirement to satisfy another

The rule: if two consecutive rounds do not move it closer, stop asking. You are no longer iterating, you are stirring.

What to do instead
  1. Change the model. The single biggest lever, and the one nobody reaches for, see below.
  2. Start a fresh conversation. A long thread full of failed attempts actively pulls it back toward those attempts. Restate the problem cleanly in a new one and it often lands first try.
  3. Change the ask, not the wording. Stop issuing corrections. Give it the end state and the test it must pass, and let it find its own route — Lesson 05.
  4. Make it diagnose. “You have tried this three times and it still fails. What do you think you are missing?” It is markedly better at critiquing its own work than at getting it right, and will often name the thing itself.
  5. Do the hard part yourself and hand it back. If one piece is the sticking point, write that piece and let it carry on. Not a defeat, a division of labour.
Usually it is the model

This is the part most people never find out, because the free tier is the only one they ever use. The gap between a small model and a frontier one is not politeness or speed. It is how many steps it can hold together before it loses the thread.

None of this reasons indefinitely. Every model has a point past which more prompting, more context and more patience produce nothing. The frontier ones reach it later, not never.

Each release pushes that boundary outward, and people reasonably expect it to keep moving. It has not disappeared yet. Knowing roughly where your own ceiling sits, and doing the work yourself when you hit it — is the actual skill.

Everyone assumes it worked first time for the other person. It did not. Assume several rounds and you will stop reading normal friction as failure.

Lesson 11

Vibe coding — now you build one

Building software without writing any, where that works, and where it bites.

CLIP 03Twenty minutes from here. Laptop or phone, free account, no install.

Bottom line You do not need a paid plan, a terminal, or any code. On the free tier of either Claude or ChatGPT you can describe a tool in plain English and get a working app running in the window beside the chat. Everyone leaves today with something that runs.
What “vibe coding” means

You describe what you want in plain English and the model writes the code. You never read it. You judge the result by whether it does the thing, and if it does not, you say what is wrong and it goes again.

The name is mocking; the practice is real. What makes it work is not that models write perfect code. It is that the feedback loop got fast enough that correctness can be tested. You do not need to read the code if you can tell in five seconds whether it behaved.

The rule that keeps you out of trouble Vibe code things where wrong is obvious and cheap. A roster that double-books someone is obvious. A calculator that returns nonsense is obvious. Something that quietly mishandles personal data, money or a password is not obvious, and that is exactly where not reading the code stops being acceptable.
A worked example: the trip app

Not a toy. A trip-coordination app for a study visit: itinerary, members, documents, offline access on a phone, backed by a spreadsheet so there was no server to run. Built by someone who does not write software for a living.

Where it genuinely bites
  • You cannot spot the subtle wrong. Obvious failures are caught by using it. Something that is right in nine cases and wrong in the tenth passes every check you will think of.
  • Anything with real data is out of scope. Credentials, payments, other people’s personal information. Not because the model is bad at it, but because “I did not read it” is not a defensible position when it goes wrong.
  • It compounds. At 200 lines nobody needs to understand the code. At 5,000 lines, nobody does, and every change risks something you cannot see.
  • It will confidently claim it is done. Always use the thing yourself. “It works now” is a prediction, not a test result.

The six-line prompt that makes this work

Vague request, vague app. The difference between a toy and something you actually use is about ninety seconds of thinking before you type. Answer six things:

  1. What it does, one sentence, as if describing it to a colleague.
  2. Who uses it, on what — phone in a corridor, or laptop at a desk? This changes everything about the layout.
  3. What goes in and what comes out, the actual fields and the actual result.
  4. The rules, the real logic. Thresholds, formulas, bands, edge cases. Be exact here; this is where people are lazy and then wonder why the output is wrong.
  5. Constraints, one page, no login, works offline, remembers my entries.
  6. Done means — how you will know it works. Give it a worked example with the answer you expect.

Then: the loop that actually gets you there
  1. Send it and let it build. Do not interrupt. Wait for something you can click.
  2. Use it like a real user. Not the happy path, the awkward one. Empty fields, silly numbers, the thing you know is a corner case.
  3. Report exactly one problem at a time. “When I leave the date blank it shows NaN.” Specific and singular. A list of five vague complaints gets you five half-fixes.
  4. Repeat until boring. Four or five rounds is normal and is not a sign you did it wrong.
  5. Then ask it to harden the thing. “What inputs would break this?” It is noticeably better at critiquing its own work than at getting it right first time.
Three habits worth stealing
  • Make it state its assumptions before it builds. Add “list the assumptions you are making, then wait for me to confirm” and you will catch the misunderstanding in ten seconds rather than after it has written the whole thing.
  • Ask for the version you did not ask for. “Show me a simpler approach you considered and rejected.” Often the rejected one is the one you wanted.
  • Keep the prompt, not just the app. The prompt is the source. If the output is lost, a good prompt rebuilds it in a minute; the app itself rebuilds nothing.
