Lesson 03

From answering to doing

How we got here, and what “agentic” actually means.

Narrated, about two minutes. Open the transcript to read along.

CLIP 01Also generated. Six seconds, no audio, about thirty cents.

Bottom line The jump that matters came when the model stopped handing you an answer and started taking actions in a loop until the job was done. You went from writing tasks to giving intent.
How it got here
BEFORE 2017
Narrow models, hand-built

Each model was trained by specialists on a labelled dataset to do a single job, such as filtering spam or classifying images. It was useless outside the task it was cut for.

2017
The transformer

A Google paper, Attention Is All You Need, proposes an architecture that scales with data and compute without plateauing. Everything since is downstream of it.

2018–2021
Scale as a strategy

Train one large model on most of the internet, then adapt it. GPT-2, GPT-3. Impressive in demos but awkward to use, and with no interface the public cared about.

NOV 2022
ChatGPT, the interface moment

The capability was mostly already there. Putting a chat box on it is what made it a household object. Remember that: the interface is what drives adoption.

2023–2024
Tools, context, modality

Models learn to call functions, read images, and hold a book-length context. Still fundamentally a single exchange: you ask, and it answers.

LATE 2024
MCP, a standard plug

Anthropic publishes the Model Context Protocol and opens it. One way to connect any model to any tool or data source, so nobody has to build a bespoke integration for each pairing. Lesson 06 covers it.

2025–2026
Agents, and the shift in your job

Reasoning models that think before answering, coding agents that work unattended for hours, skills, agent teams, cloud runners. Your work moves from writing the steps to writing the intent and checking the result.

What “agentic” actually means

The word does a lot of marketing work. Here is the honest ladder. Most people sit on rung one and assume that is the ceiling. Click each rung.

L1 ChatbotYou ask · it answers · you do the work OPEN

It produces text. Nothing happens in the world. You still open the file, check the maths and send the email. A very fast writer with no hands.

Looks like: “Draft me a paragraph on X.” You copy it out.
L2 CopilotIt suggests inside your tool · you still drive OPEN

It sits where you work and completes what you started. Faster, but the loop is unchanged: it proposes, you accept or reject, every time. You are the bottleneck by design.

Looks like: autocomplete in your editor, Smart Compose in mail.
L3 Tool useIt can reach out · one step at a time OPEN

You hand it a set of things it is allowed to call: search the web, read this file, query that sheet. It picks the right one and uses it. Still one hop: you ask, it fetches, it answers.

Looks like: “What is in my calendar on Thursday?” and it looks it up.
L4 AgenticYou give intent · it loops until done OPEN

You state an outcome and the constraints. It picks the steps, uses tools, looks at what came back, notices it was wrong, and keeps going until the outcome holds or it gets stuck. You review the finished result.

Four things make it agentic, and all four have to be present: a goal to reach, tools it may actually use, a loop where it sees the consequence of its own last move, and memory of what it already tried.

Looks like: “Work out why the sync broke last Tuesday and fix it.” Forty minutes later: a diagnosis, a patch, and a list of what it could not verify.

You appear only twice: at the start and at the end. Everything between is the loop.

What you actually are now

The most useful way to think about an agent is as a staff officer: capable, fast, tireless, well-read, and wholly dependent on you for direction and for judgement about what matters.

So your job is the one you already know: commanding. You set intent, bound it, then review the product before it goes anywhere. That review is now the main thing you are paid for.

The part that should worry you

A staff officer who is wrong usually shows it: they hesitate, hedge and come back with questions. An agent that is wrong reads exactly like an agent that is right, so the signals you spent a career learning to read are not available here.

Because it loops, an agent can stay confidently wrong for forty minutes at a stretch. It will invent a source, delete the wrong file, or declare success on something it never checked.

The discipline is the same as any delegation: state the constraints, make it show its working, and check the output yourself before it leaves your hands. Never let an agent be the last pair of eyes on anything carrying your name.

Day 1 · Lab

Lab 1.1: Delegate by intent

Rewrite three of your own requests as intent, bounds and a test.

45 min Hands-on 7 checks
What you produce

Three rewritten requests, each with an end state, constraints and a test you could apply to the output.

Steps
  1. Open whichever assistant you already use. Write down the last three things you asked it to do, in the words you actually used. Do not tidy them.
  2. For each one, answer three questions in writing (Lesson 05 goes into them properly). End state: what is true when this is done? Constraints: word count, sources, format, what it must not do. Test: how will you both know it worked?
  3. Send the rewritten version in a fresh conversation, so the old one cannot prime it.
  4. Put the two outputs side by side. Note specifically which part of the output moved and why. A general “it was better” is too vague to learn from.
  5. Add the line “list your assumptions and wait for me to confirm” and send a fourth request with it. Count how many assumptions it names that you had not thought of.

If the rewritten version produces the same output, your end state is probably still a task in disguise. “Write a 500-word summary” is a task. “I can walk into Thursday’s tutorial able to defend a position on this” is an end state.

Day 1 · Lab

Lab 1.3: The sanitisation drill

Practise the line before you are near anything that matters.

30 min Hands-on 6 checks
What you produce

A sanitised version of a real document, plus a written rule for yourself about what never goes in.

Steps
  1. Use a personal document, such as a holiday itinerary, a club’s meeting notes or a training plan for a race. Or write a realistic fake. Work documents do not go into a public AI tool, sanitised or not, unless your organisation has approved that tool for that use.
  2. Make a copy. In the copy, replace every name with a letter, every unit with a placeholder, every date with a relative one, and every real figure with an invented one that keeps the same shape.
  3. Read the sanitised copy cold. If it still identifies a person, a place or an activity, you have not finished.
  4. Only now put the sanitised copy into a tool and ask it to do the real task.
  5. Write your own rule in one sentence, and keep it somewhere you will see it. Mine is: if I would not email it to a vendor, I do not prompt it.

The common failure is replacing names but leaving a detail that identifies just as well: a unique location, an unusual piece of equipment, a date everyone would recognise. Shape matters as much as content.

Lesson 05

Skills: teaching it your standards

How to stop re-explaining your house style every single time.

Narrated, about two minutes. Open the transcript to read along.

CLIP 02Eight things plugging into one core. That is Lesson 06 in one picture.

Bottom line A skill is a folder of written instructions the model loads only when the task matches. Write your standard down once and it applies every time, to everyone you share it with. This is the most useful thing in the whole course, and few people use it.
What a skill actually is

A skill is a folder holding written instructions. It sits dormant until a task matches its description. Then the model loads it and follows it. Loading happens on demand, so you can keep two hundred skills and only the relevant one ever enters the conversation.

  • Why you care: every standard you repeat (a house format, a checklist, a marking rubric, a brief structure) becomes something you write once and never explain again.
  • Mine: an essay auditor holding the grading criteria and my tutor’s past feedback. I say “audit this” and it runs twenty checks I would otherwise forget.
  • They are plain text, so anyone can read and edit them. Hand your skill to your team and everyone works to the same standard. That is where a team gains most.

Two hundred skills cost you nothing until one matches.

Prompting is dead

“Prompt engineering” was a workaround for models that could not follow ordinary instructions. They can now, and that skill has largely expired.

What replaced it is a different job. You stop writing steps and start stating the end state, the constraints, and how you will judge the result. It works out the route.

The old way

INSTRUCTING · YOU OWN EVERY STEP

  • “Act as an expert analyst. First, summarise the attached paper in 200 words. Use formal academic English…”
  • You specify the sequence, so the output can only be as good as your plan
  • Every new task needs the whole instruction written out again
  • It stops the moment it finishes your last step, right or wrong

The agentic way

DELEGATING · YOU OWN THE OUTCOME

  • “I need to know whether this paper undermines my thesis. Tell me what would change my mind.”
  • It picks the steps, and will take ones you would not have thought of
  • Your standards live in a skill, so you never restate them
  • It keeps going until the outcome holds, or tells you it is stuck

Three things to put in every delegation, and they are the same three you would give a capable subordinate:

  1. The end state. What is true when this is done? For example, “I can walk into the tutorial able to defend a position on this.” That describes the result and leaves the method open.
  2. The constraints. Word count, sources it may use, house format, what it must not do. These set the bounds and leave the route to it.
  3. The test. How will you both know it worked? A worked example, a check it must pass, a question it must be able to answer.
Why this will feel familiar to an officer You already know how to do this. It is mission command: intent, constraints, freedom of action, back-brief. The only new part is the subordinate: a very fast machine that will not tell you when it is out of its depth unless you ask.

Before you read on, answer this for yourself: how do you brief a capable junior you cannot supervise directly? Whatever you just thought is the right answer here too.

What one looks like

It is a plain text file in a folder. The top lines say what it is and when to use it, and that is all the model reads until a matching task arrives. A real one, cut down:

--- name: brief-format description: Use when drafting any staff brief, deck or slide pack. --- Open every section with a one-line bottom line in bold. Keep slide text telegraphic. Drop articles and the verb "is". # "The plan is to book the range on Thursday" → "Book range Thursday" Never use "X, not Y" phrasing. State the claim, then the evidence. Speaking notes in full sentences; slide text in fragments.

That is the whole mechanism. The description line is the important part. It is how the model decides whether this skill is relevant to what you just asked. If the description is vague, the skill never fires.

Tightens padded prose by cutting stock habits: puffed-up significance, triads everywhere, the “not X, but Y” cadence, the em-dash habit, the stock closing paragraph about challenges and future prospects.

It works from a documented catalogue of these patterns, which is why it beats telling the model to “write more naturally”: that instruction gives it nothing specific to act on. Get it from GitHub at blader/humanizer.

A warning about both

Neither makes AI writing undetectable. Their job is to make your own prose less padded and less formulaic.

If the real question is “can I pass this off as mine”, the answer for assessed work is no. Your institution’s rules apply whatever any tool does.

Writing your own: the fast route

A skill written from a blank page comes out generic. Do the task once properly and let the corrections become the skill.

  1. Do the job once, out loud. Work through a real essay or deck with the model and correct it as you go. Every “no, we always do it this way” is a future rule.
  2. Then say: “turn everything you just learnt into a skill.” It writes the file, including the description line. This is the whole trick, and almost nobody does it.
  3. Cut half of what it wrote. It over-generalises. Keep the rules that are specifically yours; delete the ones that are just good practice, which it already knows.
  4. Sharpen the description line. That line alone decides whether the skill ever fires. Name the trigger: “Use when drafting or reviewing any assessed essay for the X programme.”
  5. Use it and fix what it misses. Mine took four or five rounds before it earned its place.
The payoff, concretely My essay-audit skill holds a marking rubric and every piece of feedback I have been given. I say “audit this” and it runs twenty checks I would otherwise forget at midnight the night before submission. I wrote none of those checks in the moment. They accumulated from corrections I made once.

If a tutor has ever given you written feedback, that feedback is the skill. Most people file it and never open it again. Put it in a skill file and it works on every assignment from now on.

Where to find them
  • Built in. Claude ships with a set: document handling, slide building, spreadsheets. Ask “what skills do you have?” and it will list them.
  • Your organisation’s catalogue. If your workplace publishes skills, they appear in your settings and install in one click.
  • Public repositories. Anthropic publishes a skills repository on GitHub, and there are community collections. Read before you install. A skill is instructions the model will follow, so treat an unknown one the way you would an unknown script.

Lab 2.1: Build a skill from your own feedback

Turn marking you have already received into something that works on every future assignment.

75 min Hands-on 8 checks
What you produce

A working skill file that catches at least three things a marker has previously pulled you up on.

Steps
  1. Find written feedback you have been given: tutor comments, a marked essay, a returned brief. Any amount will do.
  2. Pull out every recurring criticism: the things said more than once, or that you suspect would be said again. Leave the one-offs aside.
  3. Start a conversation and work through a real piece of writing with the model, correcting it as you go against those criticisms. Be explicit: “no, we always do it this way, because...”
  4. When you have corrected it four or five times, say: “turn everything you just learnt into a skill, including the description line.”
  5. Read what it produced and cut half. Keep what is specific to you; delete generic good practice, which it already knows.
  6. Sharpen the description so it names the trigger: “Use when drafting or reviewing any assessed essay for [your programme].”
  7. Save it where your tool loads skills. In Claude, upload it under Settings, then Capabilities, then Skills; Claude Code reads a skills folder in your project or home directory. Where custom skills are not available on your plan, paste the file into a Project’s instructions instead, which does most of the same job.
  8. Test it on a different piece of writing. Does it fire without being asked? Does it catch the things you built it for?

If it never fires, the problem is in the description line, because that is all the model reads before it decides. Write it as a trigger condition that names the task, starting “Use when”.

Lesson 06

MCP, connectors, and where work happens

How the model reaches your tools, and the difference between local and cloud.

Narrated, about two minutes. Open the transcript to read along.

Bottom line MCP is a standard plug that lets any model use any tool. A connector is one of those plugs with the wiring already done. And “local” does not mean private. That distinction matters most here.
MCP: one standard plug for every tool

Model Context Protocol. Anthropic published it in late 2024 and gave it away. The problem it solves is unglamorous and enormous.

An MCP server offers three kinds of thing: tools it can do, resources it can read, and prompts it can follow. The model is told what is available and decides when to reach for it. The analogy everyone uses is USB-C, and for once the analogy is fair.

Connectors: MCP with the wiring done
GmailDriveDocsNotionSlackGitHubZotero

A connector is a prepackaged MCP server for a service you already use. You click it, sign in once through the normal login screen, and tick what it is allowed to touch. You do not need to edit files or open a terminal.

  • Typical list: Gmail, Google Drive and Calendar, Notion, Slack, GitHub, Figma, Canva.
  • Grant narrowly. Read-only beats read-write unless you need it to write. It can do anything you permit, including the things you did not picture it doing.
  • Revoke when the job is done. A connector you set up for one afternoon should not still be live next term.
Mods: the one that landed in October

Anthropic shipped mods on 1 October 2026. They are new enough that almost nobody on your team will have seen one, and they change what a plugin is allowed to be.

Until now a plugin bundled things the model uses: skills, subagents (helper agents it hands a sub-task to), hooks and MCP servers. A hook was a handler fired at a set point in a session, such as before a tool runs or when a task ends. It could block an action or post a short message, but it could not draw anything of its own. A mod is a plugin whose hooks are written as JavaScript functions running inside the session, and those functions can draw. Mods run in Claude Code, so you need the $20 Pro plan or above (Lesson 04).

  • A pane. A panel beside your work, with its own buttons, refreshing on its own.
  • A band above the prompt. One line that is always there. Project health, a count of what is overdue, which branch you are on.
  • A status line and toasts. Quiet notes, and a nudge when something finishes.
  • Its own commands, defined in the module itself with no separate command files.

It also hot-reloads: you edit the module and the running session picks up the change, so you see each edit straight away without restarting.

Local vs cloud: where the work happens

This trips people up constantly, and the distinction is simpler than it sounds: it is about where the hands are. The brain (the model itself) is a remote service either way. What changes is what it can reach.

Local

ON YOUR MACHINE · e.g. Claude Code, Codex CLI

  • Sees your actual files, folders and version history
  • Runs real commands: installs, tests, builds, scripts
  • Reaches things only your machine can reach
  • Nothing is uploaded except what it chooses to read
  • It can break things. Keep backups.
  • Needs installing and a little terminal comfort
  • Dies when your laptop sleeps

Cloud

ON THEIR SERVERS · e.g. claude.ai, chatgpt.com

  • Nothing to install: a browser or a phone is enough
  • Sandboxed, so it cannot wreck your machine
  • Keeps running while you are away from the desk
  • Same conversation on every device
  • Sees only what you upload or connect
  • Cannot touch your local files or network
  • Everything you paste leaves your building

Which one for the job in front of you?

The rule that has not changed

Local does not mean private. The model still runs on someone else’s servers. Local only means the hands are on your machine. Every file it reads locally is sent off to be processed, same as anything you paste into a browser.

So the rule is the boring one: nothing classified, nothing marked, no personal data of other people, into any of these services. That applies on every tier, including local tools. If you would not email it to a vendor, do not prompt it.

Everything in this course is unclassified, open-source and built on my own time and my own account. Keep it that way and you will not have a bad afternoon.

Lesson 07

Research that stands up

Finding real sources, checking they say what you claim, and digging past the summary.

Narrated, about two minutes. Open the transcript to read along.

Bottom line A chat model will invent a citation that looks perfect: a plausible author, journal and year for a paper that does not exist. The entire discipline here is one rule: every citation comes from a search result you can click. If the model wrote it from memory, treat it as invented until you find it.
Why invented citations happen

The model generates text likely to follow what came before, and a citation-shaped string is extremely likely to follow a claim. So it produces one, correctly formatted, whether or not any such paper exists.

It happens most when you ask for something that ought to exist. Ask for “a study showing X” and if none is in memory, the likeliest next text is still a citation.

What this costs you

A fabricated reference in assessed work is an academic-integrity problem, and trivially caught: a marker only has to search the title.

The commoner failure is worse. A real paper cited for a claim it does not make survives a quick check and still collapses under a marker who knows the literature.

The workflow that holds up
  1. Map the ground first, with a tool that searches. Use Perplexity or a Scholar search, something that returns real results with real links. A chat window working from memory gives you titles you cannot click. At this stage you only want to know “what exists and who is canonical here?”
  2. Go and find who disagrees. Take your position and search for its strongest published critic by name. A rubric rewards engaging a real opponent and punishes a straw man, and the model will happily build you a straw man if you do not insist otherwise.
  3. Read the actual source for anything load-bearing. A summary can drop the qualification that matters. If a claim is holding up a section of your argument, you read the page it came from.
  4. Check every citation twice. Once that the paper exists. Again that the specific claim you hung on it is the claim it makes. This second check is where the marks actually are.
  5. Track where each source entered. Note what you took from each and why it is in the essay at all. Any source you cannot justify should be cut: a marker can tell when a bibliography has been padded.

LiteratureGoogle Scholar

Returns actual papers with actual citation counts. The useful part is who has cited this since: that forward link is how you find the rebuttal to a paper you were about to lean on.

Use scholar.google.com in your browser and paste the result links into the chat. Connecting Scholar as a tool needs a third-party setup and is optional. Either way the model works from results you can open, and that is what separates a real reference from an invented one.

That is a meaningful shift: the answer is grounded in your own reading list. Connecting Zotero as a tool needs a third-party setup and is optional. Without it, upload the PDFs you need to a chat or a Gemini Notebook.

WebPerplexity

Fast orientation with citations on every claim. Best for the first hour of a topic you know nothing about: who the names are, what the debate is and which terms matter.

Ask it“What is the main line of criticism against this position?”

A summary request gets you a summary. Even an accurate one adds little, because everyone else gets the same one. The value is in questions that force a judgement.

Lesson 08

Decks, documents and your own files

Giving it a format to follow, and pointing it at the folders you already have.

Narrated, about two minutes. Open the transcript to read along.

Bottom line A deck comes out usable when you give it your format plus your content, because the format settles what a bare request for “a deck on X” leaves it to guess. Once it can read your folders, it can also answer questions about everything you already have.
The one move that makes decks work

Ask it to fill a format you have already decided, and keep the design decisions for yourself. Design is where it is weakest, and your house style is non-negotiable anyway.

So hand it the rules explicitly. A real set, from the deck standard I work to:

FORMAT 16:9. Minimum 8.5pt type. Never split a point across two slides. Trim it to fit one. Yellow bar across the top of each slide = the bottom line, one sentence. SLIDE TEXT Telegraphic. Drop articles and the verb "is". # "The plan is to book the range on Thursday" → "Book range Thursday" No rhetorical antithesis. Never "X, not Y". SPEAKING NOTES Full sentences, written to be read aloud. LENGTH 35–40 visible slides. No repeated content across sections.

That is thirty seconds of typing and it is the difference between a deck you rebuild from scratch and one you tidy. Better still, put it in a skill (Lesson 05) and never type it again.

  • Give it an existing deck as the reference. “Match this one’s structure and tone” works far better than any description you can write.
  • Write the content before the slides. Settle the argument in prose, then ask it to lay that out. Decks built before the argument exists are where filler comes from.
  • Make it justify the slide count. “What would you cut to lose five slides?” produces a tighter deck than asking for a shorter one.

Pointing it at your own folders

This is the capability people underestimate most. Running locally (Lesson 06), it can read a whole directory: every file in it, with the folder structure intact.

That changes the kind of question you can ask:

  • “Read every PDF in this folder and build me a thematic index.” Forty readings become a map of which sources speak to which theme.
  • “Which of these documents contradict each other?” Across a folder of your own notes, readings and drafts, this catches things no one person would spot.
  • “Find every file that mentions this and tell me where it came from.” Search that matches on meaning, so it finds the file even when the wording differs.
  • “These are my last six essays and their feedback. What do I keep getting wrong?” Only the whole folder can answer it, and the answer tells you what to fix in your next essay.
  • “Reorganise this folder and tell me what you moved.” Useful, and exactly the kind of thing to make it propose before it acts.
Two rules before you point it at a folder

Everything it reads leaves your machine. Local means its hands are local; the processing still happens on the provider’s servers. A folder of personal data or marked material is not a folder you point anything at.

Make it propose before it moves anything. Add “show me what you would change and wait for me to confirm”. Reading changes nothing. An agent that misunderstands your folder structure can rename or delete the wrong files.

Lesson 09

Academic work that holds up

Essays, reflections, briefs and a thesis, and which parts of each you can safely delegate.

Bottom line The model’s real value in assessed work is attacking your own argument before somebody else does. The instinct is to ask it to write the thing, and that produces competent, average work that a marker has read forty times.
Read this first

Every institution now has a position on AI in assessed work, and they differ sharply. Some expect a declaration, some permit it at specific stages, some prohibit it outright.

Find your programme’s rule and follow it. Nothing on this page is a way around that, and “I did not know” has never been a defence.

Essays

Ask it for resistance: have it find the objections and gaps in a draft you wrote yourself.

Most people skip the audit pass, yet it is the step most likely to move you up a band. Run these four questions on your own finished draft:

  1. “Which paragraph is doing the least work?” Then cut it. It will name one, and it is usually right.
  2. “Where have I asserted something without evidence?” Orphan claims are the most common thing in marker feedback and the easiest to fix once named.
  3. “Argue the opposite as strongly as you can.” If the counter-case is stronger than your case, you have found out in time.
  4. “Where does my argument leap?” Ask it to find every place you move from X to Y without showing the step between.
Reflections

Reflective writing is the one form where delegating the writing is both obvious and self-defeating. The marker is assessing whether you thought about it. A model cannot have had your week.

So invert it and ask it to interview you:

I have to write a reflection on [the thing that happened]. Do not write it. Ask me eight questions, one at a time, that would make me say something I have not already thought. Push back when an answer is vague. When we are done, show me the three strongest things I said, in my own words.

That gets you a reflection built from your own sentences, which is both the honest version and reliably the better one. It also surfaces the thing you were avoiding, which is usually the material the marker is looking for.

Briefs and presentations

Covered in the previous lesson, with one addition specific to assessed work. A brief is marked on the judgement it shows, so the common failure is a thorough deck that never makes a recommendation.

  • Write the recommendation first, in one sentence, before you ask for a single slide. If you cannot, you are not ready to build the deck.
  • Give it the format you already work to, then let it fill it. Your house rules go in a skill once.
  • Ask what a hostile audience would attack. Then answer it on a slide before anyone raises it.
Deep research

Most assistants now have a long-form research mode that runs for several minutes, searches widely and returns something report-shaped with citations. It is useful, and it is routinely misused.

Good at

USE IT HERE

  • Mapping an unfamiliar field fast
  • Finding the names you should know
  • Gathering candidate sources to check
  • Surfacing a view you had not met

Bad at

DO NOT TRUST IT HERE

  • Judging which source is authoritative
  • Noticing that two of them contradict
  • Weighting evidence by quality
  • Being right about anything you cannot check

Treat its output as a reading list with commentary. Open the sources that matter before you rely on any of it. The report is where your research starts.

A thesis, or anything long

At fifteen thousand words the problem changes. The hard part becomes coherence across the whole: whether chapter six still agrees with chapter two.

  1. Keep a claim ledger. One line per claim: where it is made and what supports it. A plain file will do. The next step brings it into each fresh conversation.
  2. Work chapter by chapter, in fresh conversations. A thread carrying your whole thesis gets worse the longer it runs. Start clean and bring the ledger.
  3. Put your conventions in a skill. Referencing style, terminology, which spelling, how you refer to your own argument. Re-explaining that at chapter nine is how drift happens.
  4. Run a coherence pass across chapters. Point it at the whole folder and ask where the chapters disagree with each other. Lesson 08 covers how.
  5. Re-audit near the end. Claims that were true in March may not have survived your own later chapters.
The honest summary Use it to map the field, check your sources and attack your argument. Do the thinking and the writing yourself, and keep the final judgement. Anything you would be embarrassed to be asked about in a viva is a thing you should have done yourself.

Lesson 11

When it goes wrong

The first output is never right. Knowing why, and when to stop pushing.

Bottom line Nobody gets what they wanted on the first try, and the people who conclude “AI cannot do this” almost always quit at round one. But iteration has limits too. When you start going in circles, the usual cause is the model you are on, and the same request often lands one tier up.
The first output is never right

Every brief you have ever given works the same way: the first version comes back and you say “not quite, more like this”. The difference is that here the turnaround is ninety seconds instead of three days, so you can afford to do it eight times.

And every round is doing real work. When you say “no, the roster needs to avoid consecutive weekends”, you are telling it something it could not have known. Each correction narrows the target.

This is also why it burns through usage so fast. Tearing the work apart and rebuilding costs roughly what the original build cost. Five rounds is five builds. That is the honest price of the speed, and it is why the usage limits in Lesson 04 bite sooner than people expect.

Worth saying out loud This course went through several substantial rebuilds. One long page became a three-day course. Whole lessons arrived after the structure was supposedly finished. That is what the work normally looks like.
Converging, or going in circles?

These look identical for the first few rounds. Learn to tell them apart, because only one of them is worth continuing.

Converging

KEEP GOING

  • Each version is different from the last
  • The thing you named as wrong is now right
  • New problems are smaller than the old ones
  • It asks you a sharp question you had not considered
  • It tells you what it is uncertain about

Circling

STOP: MORE PROMPTING WILL NOT FIX IT

  • A bug you already fixed comes back
  • Same answer, reworded, twice running
  • It agrees with every correction and changes nothing
  • It apologises more than it edits
  • It quietly drops a requirement to satisfy another

The rule: if two consecutive rounds do not move it closer, stop asking. Past that point each new round just stirs the same failed attempts.

What to do instead
  1. Change the model. It is the fix people try last (see below).
  2. Start a fresh conversation. A long thread full of failed attempts actively pulls it back towards those attempts. Restate the problem cleanly in a new one and it often lands first try.
  3. Change the ask itself. Stop issuing corrections. Give it the end state and the test it must pass, and let it find its own route (Lesson 05).
  4. Make it diagnose. “You have tried this three times and it still fails. What do you think you are missing?” It is markedly better at critiquing its own work than at getting it right, and will often name the thing itself.
  5. Do the hard part yourself and hand it back. If one piece is the sticking point, write that piece and let it carry on. That is a sensible division of labour.

None of this reasons indefinitely. Every model has a point past which extra prompting, context and patience produce nothing. The frontier ones reach it later.

Each release pushes that boundary outward, and people reasonably expect it to keep moving. It has not disappeared yet. The actual skill is knowing roughly where your own ceiling sits, and doing the work yourself when you hit it.

Everyone assumes it worked first time for the other person. It did not. Assume several rounds and you will stop reading normal friction as failure.

Lesson 12

Vibe coding: now you build one

Building software without writing any, where that works, and where it bites.

CLIP 03Day 3 ends with something live, built on a laptop or phone with a free account and nothing to install.

Bottom line You do not need a paid plan, a terminal, or any code. On the free tier of either Claude or ChatGPT you can describe a tool in plain English and get a working app running in the window beside the chat. By the end of Day 3 you will have something that runs.
What “vibe coding” means

You describe what you want in plain English and the model writes the code. You never read it. You judge the result by whether it does the thing, and if it does not, you say what is wrong and it goes again.

Despite the mocking name, the practice is real. Models still write imperfect code; what makes it work is that the feedback loop got fast enough that correctness can be tested. You do not need to read the code if you can tell in five seconds whether it behaved.

The rule that keeps you out of trouble Vibe code things where wrong is obvious and cheap. A roster that double-books someone is obviously wrong, and so is a calculator that returns nonsense. Something that quietly mishandles personal data, money or a password is not obvious, and that is exactly where not reading the code stops being acceptable.
A worked example: the trip app

A real trip-coordination app for a study visit: itinerary, members, documents, offline access on a phone, backed by a spreadsheet so there was no server to run. Built by someone who does not write software for a living.

Where it bites
  • You cannot spot the subtle wrong. Obvious failures are caught by using it. Something that is right in nine cases and wrong in the tenth passes every check you will think of.
  • Anything with real data is out of scope. Credentials, payments, other people’s personal information. The problem is accountability: “I did not read it” is not a defensible position when it goes wrong.
  • It compounds. At 200 lines you can get by without understanding the code. By 5,000 lines nobody understands it, and every change risks something you cannot see.
  • It will confidently claim it is done. Always use the thing yourself. “It works now” is only a prediction until you have tested it.

The six-line prompt that makes this work

A vague request gets you a vague app. The difference between a toy and something you actually use is about ninety seconds of thinking before you type. Answer six things:

  1. What it does: one sentence, as if describing it to a colleague.
  2. Who uses it, on what: phone in a corridor, or laptop at a desk? This changes everything about the layout.
  3. What goes in and what comes out: the actual fields and the actual result.
  4. The rules: the real logic. Thresholds, formulas, bands, edge cases. Be exact here; this is where people are lazy and then wonder why the output is wrong.
  5. Constraints: one page, no login, works offline, remembers my entries.
  6. Done means: how you will know it works. Give it a worked example with the answer you expect.

Then: the loop that actually gets you there
  1. Send it and let it build. Do not interrupt. Wait for something you can click.
  2. Use it like a real user. Take the awkward path: empty fields, silly numbers, the thing you know is a corner case.
  3. Report exactly one problem at a time. “When I leave the date blank it shows NaN.” Specific and singular. A list of five vague complaints gets you five half-fixes.
  4. Repeat until boring. Four or five rounds is normal and is not a sign you did it wrong.
  5. Then ask it to harden the thing. “What inputs would break this?” It is noticeably better at critiquing its own work than at getting it right first time.
Three habits worth stealing
  • Make it state its assumptions before it builds. Add “list the assumptions you are making, then wait for me to confirm” and you will catch the misunderstanding in ten seconds, before it has written the whole thing.
  • Ask for the version you did not ask for. “Show me a simpler approach you considered and rejected.” Often the rejected one is the one you wanted.
  • Keep the prompt as well as the app. The prompt is the source. If the app is lost, a good prompt rebuilds it in a minute.
Keep it unclassified

Build with invented names and made-up numbers. The exercise is about the technique, so an invented roster teaches as much as your real one. Anything you type into a free account is on somebody else’s server by the time you hit enter.

Lesson 13

Design thinking, with AI on the team

What a technopreneurship module and one team project taught me, and how I expect staff work to use design thinking with AI.

Bottom line Lesson 12 taught you to build. This lesson covers deciding what to build and checking afterwards whether it was worth the effort. Design thinking is the loop: watch the people with the problem, name it, try the cheapest fix, test it on someone new. AI helps at every stage, and testing, the final stage, carries the most weight.
What the module was about

On the staff course I took a module on the technopreneurial mindset. It means thinking like someone who starts a company, whatever your job: spot a problem, then take a cheap first step with what you have and check whether you were right.

Inside an organisation this is called intrapreneurship, a term usually traced to Gifford Pinchot. The military case for it is that the people closest to a problem tend to see the fix first.

AI lowers the cost of acting on it. A working prototype used to need a team and a budget. Now one officer with a laptop can put a running tool on the table where a proposal would once have gone.

The loop

The version of design thinking in this lesson has five stages, taken from the Stanford d.school: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test.

You go round more than once. A test usually sends you back to the start.

It is tempting to skip the first two stages. They can build now, so they start at stage four. A tool built for a problem you only imagined will probably not fit the real one. Go and watch someone do the job. Write the problem in one sentence. If you cannot, you are not ready to open the AI.

Plan from what you have

Saras Sarasvathy set out two ways of planning. Causation fixes a goal and plans backwards from it. Effectuation starts from the means you already hold and lets the goal take shape as you go.

Causation suits stable problems you can forecast, and effectuation suits fast, uncertain ones.

Causation fits problems you can forecast. Effectuation fits fast, uncertain problems, the kind this module covered. Its five habits are to start from your own skills and contacts, risk only what you can afford to lose, bring in partners as you go, use unexpected events as inputs to the plan, and spend effort on what you control.

AI helps in two ways. It enlarges what you start with, because you can look up and combine far more than you personally know. It cuts the cost of a first attempt to a few hours, so the loss you can afford buys more tries. It carries one risk. A confident AI forecast can pull you back into causation on a problem nobody can forecast.

Four more ideas from the module

ProblemsWicked problems and BANI

Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber called a problem wicked when it has no clean definition and no final answer, because every attempt changes it. Jamais Cascio’s BANI (brittle, anxious, non-linear, incomprehensible) describes the conditions around such problems.

Use it forChoosing how to work. On a wicked problem you experiment and reframe, because one big plan will not hold.

TeamsSafe, and blunt

Amy Edmondson popularised psychological safety: people can raise ideas and mistakes without being punished. Gary Pisano adds a condition. Safety on its own is not enough. Pisano pairs it with frank, respectful criticism that travels up the chain as well as down.

Ask itTell a model to attack your plan. It tends to agree with you unless told otherwise.

ExperimentsTests that can kill the idea

Pisano’s advice is to design the cheap test that could kill your idea, and to set the kill criteria before you run it. Failure is productive when it teaches you something worth the cost.

Watch outAI makes it easy to write a persuasive report on a weak result. Fix the criteria first.

Outside helpOpen innovation

Henry Chesbrough’s idea is to use outside ideas and outside routes to market alongside your own. In practice, scouting and partnerships can involve outside parties, and classified capabilities stay in-house.

Use it forDeciding what an outside AI tool may see.
My project

My team’s project began with one piece of paperwork. Every overseas military flight needs clearance, and each place you fly to wants it on its own form, with its own fields and file format. A planning office was typing the same mission details into form after form, and redoing the lot whenever the plan changed. I describe it in general terms, with no names, figures or document text.

We asked around. Other units had the same shape of problem: one form, many contributors, one person copying inputs across by hand, and sources held in different places. Daily headcount returns and movement paperwork looked just like it. We took them to be a class of small, recurring admin jobs, each too small to reach the front of a central digitalisation queue, though we had no queue or cost evidence to show it.

We tested the idea by building. Our brief to an AI coding agent was roughly this: here are the templates, let me enter the mission once, give me back each place’s own form, and make it work offline. The agent worked out the fields the forms shared and generated the forms. We checked the first output against a form that had been accepted. A second app, for a different job, was built the same way without a budget or a contractor. Do not copy this step with real work material. What an agent may be shown is the first boundary under “Where the line is”.

Our claim was that process owners could build first versions with agentic AI while the central technical offices kept assurance and lifecycle ownership. We proposed a short course and a gated pilot, and declared in the report that an AI chat tool had helped us restructure and tighten the text.

What the examiner and the audit raised

The examiner asked what we added to what already exists. A pre-submission audit of our report raised the points below.

  • Something like it may already exist. The examiner named commercial form-automation products, AI courses and in-house digital specialists. Whether any of them closes the gap had not been tested. Saying the requirement is custom does not answer that. A custom requirement is a reason to write the requirement down, then check whether a configured product already meets it.
  • Estimates reported as results. Our headline workload figure multiplied a per-form time estimate by the size of our whole template library, as if one route needed every form in it. The scope of a typical route had not been established. The app’s own time-saved counter was another estimate. We had told the audience in the pitch that the counter was not a measurement, and the written report still led with an estimate of its own. Our line about building in days compared a prototype with delivered, assured software.
  • Acceptance by the receiving side was unproven. In a live check, the sampled values were right, and one output still came back as a blank form to finish by hand. Our scale-up gate was a single overall accuracy score, and the audit pointed out that an aggregate pass rate can hide failures in the fields that matter most.
  • Two apps from one team. They show the method worked for us. It was untested whether it works for builders who have never seen it.

We had shown that something useful could be built fast. Time saved, use by other builders and comparison with the alternatives were all untested. The audit asked us to keep three numbers apart: the baseline effort, the effort we estimated we could avoid, and the saving we had measured.

A prototype built in days shows that something is possible. Whether it pays needs a timed baseline and a later measurement. AI has made building cheap, so the remaining effort is in measuring whether the tool pays.
